Vocabulary
8 terms for talking about the business side of gaming — money, labor, and the language of the industry
0 1
MONETIZE
Verb

Collocations: monetize the player base · aggressive monetization · monetization strategy · ways to monetize · monetized through ads

To find a way to make money from something you've built — usually by adding purchases, ads, or subscriptions directly into how it works. Ex: A free app might monetize by showing ads between levels or selling extra lives.

Example

"It's becoming harder to find a mobile game that hasn't been monetized through ads, loot boxes, or some kind of subscription model."

0 2
CRUNCH
Noun / Verb

Collocations: brutal crunch · crunch culture · crunch period · go into crunch · months of crunch

An intense stretch of overtime — sometimes weeks or months long — that teams go through right before a game has to ship. Ex: Some studios still expect staff to work nights and weekends during crunch, even though it's been criticized for years.

Example

"By the final month of crunch, half the team was sleeping at the office just to hit the release date."

0 3
CONSOLIDATION
Noun

Collocations: industry consolidation · wave of consolidation · market consolidation · ongoing consolidation · consolidation of power

A trend where smaller companies get bought up or merged into bigger ones, until just a handful of giants end up running most of the show. Ex: Consolidation is part of why so many studios you used to think of as independent now belong to the same parent company.

Example

"Consolidation in the industry means a handful of publishers now control most of the big franchises we grew up with."

0 4
LIVE-SERVICE
Adjective

Collocations: live-service model · live-service game · live-service title · shift to live-service · live-service economy

Describes a game that's designed to keep changing after launch — new seasons, new content, new things to buy — instead of being finished and left alone. Ex: A live-service game might look totally different a year after release, with new characters, maps, or events added regularly.

Example

"They redesigned the entire game as a live-service title, which means it'll keep getting new content — and new ways to spend money — for years."

0 5
BACKLASH
Noun

Collocations: swift backlash · public backlash · spark backlash · face backlash · backlash online

A sudden wave of public anger or criticism, usually after a decision that a lot of people feel went too far. Ex: A pricing change can spark so much backlash that a company ends up reversing it within a day.

Example

"The backlash was so intense that the studio had to walk back the changes within forty-eight hours of launch."

0 6
LAYOFFS
Noun

Collocations: mass layoffs · round of layoffs · announce layoffs · hit by layoffs · layoffs across the studio

Job cuts that happen when a company decides to shrink its workforce — often after a merger, a disappointing launch, or pressure to save money. Ex: Layoffs can hit a studio even after one of its games becomes a huge hit, which a lot of people find hard to understand.

Example

"Another round of layoffs hit the studio last week, just months after the game shipped to record sales."

0 7
FRANCHISE
Noun

Collocations: beloved franchise · franchise fatigue · long-running franchise · reboot a franchise · franchise installment

A series of games that share characters, settings, or a brand — valuable to a company because fans keep coming back for more. Ex: A successful franchise can spin off into movies, merchandise, and spreadsheets full of profit projections.

Example

"The franchise has been rebooted so many times that newer fans barely recognize the characters from the original games."

0 8
EARLY ACCESS
Noun

Collocations: launch in early access · early access release · buy into early access · early access period · leave early access

A system where players can buy and play a game before it's finished — sometimes helping fund development and shape the final version through feedback. Ex: Some games stay in early access for years, slowly adding features as the small team behind them keeps working.

Example

"I bought it in early access last year, and honestly, it's barely changed since then."

Language Focus
8 phrases for recognizing — and naming — the language companies use to soften difficult news
0 1
"It was decided that…"
Passive Decision-Maker Removal

Hides exactly who made a call. The decision exists, but nobody seems to have actually made it — which is often the point.

Example

"It was decided that the project would be discontinued, with resources reallocated to other priorities."

0 2
"Certain roles have been impacted…"
Euphemistic Impact Phrase

A softened way of saying "people lost their jobs" without saying it directly — "impacted" does a lot of quiet work here.

Example

"As part of this transition, certain roles across the studio have been impacted."

0 3
"Going forward, our focus will be…"
Forward-Looking Pivot

Steers attention away from whatever just went wrong and toward a brighter-sounding plan — a classic move right after bad news.

Example

"Going forward, our focus will be on delivering experiences that resonate with our most engaged communities."

0 4
"We hear you, and we're listening…"
Acknowledgment Without Commitment

Sounds responsive without promising anything specific — useful for calming people down while buying time to figure out what to actually do.

Example

"We hear you, and we're listening — your feedback on the new pricing model has been invaluable."

0 5
"The decision was not made lightly…"
Gravity Signal

Adds emotional weight to an announcement — suggesting real struggle behind the scenes — even when the outcome for the people affected doesn't change at all.

Example

"The decision was not made lightly, and we recognize the impact this will have on those affected."

0 6
"Let's call this what it actually is…"
Direct Reframe (Critical Register)

The opposite move — a critic or journalist stripping away the soft corporate phrasing and naming the situation in plain terms.

Example

"Let's call this what it actually is — a company posting record profits while cutting hundreds of jobs."

0 7
"Who, exactly, made this call?"
Accountability Question

Pushes back directly against vague, passive phrasing by demanding a name and a reason — the question corporate language is usually designed to dodge.

Example

"Who, exactly, made this call, and why weren't the people affected told before the press release went out?"

0 8
"Read between the lines…"
Decoding Cue

Signals that the literal wording of a statement isn't the whole story — and invites the listener to work out what's really being said.

Example

"If you read between the lines of that statement, what they're really saying is that more cuts are coming."

💡 Language Focus — Passive Voice & the Language of Corporate Distance

When companies have difficult news to share — job cuts, cancelled projects, price hikes — they often reach for language that spreads responsibility thin, or removes it completely. Passive voice, vague nouns, and forward-looking pivots all do the same job: they let an announcement sound calm and official, without anyone having to say "we did this, and here's why."

Active voice

Form: subject + verb + object
Signal: names who did what
"The company laid off 200 people."
Direct and clear — assigns responsibility plainly, which is exactly why it's rare in official statements about bad news.

Passive voice

Form: object + be + past participle (+ by…)
Signal: softens or removes the actor
"200 roles were eliminated."
Standard in official announcements — sounds neutral and procedural, and quietly drops the question of who decided.

Nominalization

Form: turning a verb into an abstract noun
Signal: maximum distance from the action
"A workforce reduction was announced."
The action becomes a "thing" that simply occurred — with no clear actor and, often, no clear scale either.

Same news, increasing distance — watch what disappears at each step

"The company laid off 200 employees this week." → active — names the actor and the action plainly
"200 employees were laid off this week." → passive — the actor disappears, but the action is still named
"It was announced this week that 200 positions would be eliminated." → passive + impersonal subject — adds another layer ("announced" by whom?)
"This week's announcement confirmed a workforce reduction affecting approximately 200 roles." → full nominalization — "layoffs" becomes an abstract "reduction," and the announcement itself becomes the subject
⚠️ Overusing passive and impersonal language doesn't just sound formal — it can sound evasive. When every sentence hides who did what, readers and critics tend to notice, and the gap between what's said and what's clearly meant becomes the story itself.
In Use
See how the same industry vocabulary sounds in a casual group chat, an industry podcast, and a piece of business journalism
Group ChatFriends texting about gaming news

"ok so apparently the studio that made that game just announced ANOTHER round of layoffs?? like the game did so well too, i don't get it. feels like every month it's a different studio getting hit. also did you see the new monetization in that other game? it's so aggressive now, i genuinely can't open a menu without something trying to sell me a bundle. honestly i think we're hitting real franchise fatigue at this point, like how many remasters do we actually need lol"

Notice how casually these huge industry topics get dropped into ordinary chat — "ANOTHER round of layoffs," "franchise fatigue" — without any explanation. In informal speech, people often assume the listener already shares the background knowledge, so the vocabulary itself does double duty as both information and shorthand for a shared frustration.
Panel DiscussionPodcast — "Who Actually Owns Gaming Now?"

"What's interesting about this latest wave of consolidation is that it's reshaping how games even get made. When three or four publishers control most of the big franchises, you start to see a much narrower range of live-service titles getting greenlit, because that's where the predictable, long-term revenue is. The risk, of course, is that the kind of crunch culture we've criticized for years just gets baked even deeper into the system, because now there's even more pressure to hit quarterly numbers."

This speaker links three big ideas — consolidation, live-service models, and crunch — into a single chain of cause and effect. That's a very C1-C2 move: rather than listing problems, they're explaining how one industry trend quietly produces another.
Trade Press"Inside the Early Access Gamble" — industry news feature

"The studio's decision to launch its flagship title in early access has drawn a mixed response from critics and players alike. While some have praised the transparency of an iterative development process shaped by community feedback, others argue that the move simply shifts the burden of testing — and the cost of crunch — onto the people least equipped to absorb it. The backlash following last month's price announcement suggests that audiences are growing increasingly skeptical of how the industry frames these decisions."

Notice the more formal, even-handed tone here — "has drawn a mixed response," "others argue" — typical of trade journalism that wants to sound balanced while still raising a pointed criticism. Compare this measured framing with the directness of the group chat above.
Register & Stance
How the same piece of industry news gets reframed — from maximum distance to zero distance — depending on who's speaking and why
ContextDistance LevelExample
Corporate press release
Official company statement		 High — maximal distance "It was decided that a number of roles would be impacted as part of a broader organizational realignment."
Industry analyst report
Formal written analysis		 High — measured "The reduction in headcount reflects a wider trend toward consolidation across the sector."
Trade journalism
Neutral, accessible		 Moderate — balanced "The studio confirmed plans to cut roughly 200 positions, citing the need to 'streamline operations.'"
Podcast commentary
Semi-formal, discursive		 Low — direct but careful "They're basically laying people off right after a record quarter — and calling it a 'realignment.'"
Social media / public debate
Informal, direct		 Minimal / None — blunt "Another studio just fired 200 people the same week they bragged about record profits."
✅Distance isn't dishonesty by default. A press release that says "roles were affected" instead of naming individuals isn't necessarily hiding anything — it's following a convention. The skill is learning to read past the convention to what's actually being said.
💡Notice how the same fact — people lost their jobs — gets reframed at every step: "impacted," "reduction in headcount," "cut positions," "laying people off," "fired." Each version signals something about who's speaking and how they want you to feel about it.
⚠️Watch for stacked distancing devices in the same sentence — passive voice plus nominalization plus vague quantifiers ("certain changes were made to certain roles affecting a number of people"). When distance piles up like that, audiences often read it as evasive rather than professional.
❌Don't assume "formal" always means "passive" or "vague." A formal analyst report can be direct and specific ("Publisher X cut 12% of its workforce") — formality is about register and precision, not about hiding the subject of the sentence.

I used to pre-order everything from the big studios — the new click to fill entry, day one, no questions asked. But somewhere along the way I started noticing the same pattern over and over: a game ships, and within weeks it's been redesigned around new ways to click to fill every little thing, like the actual game was just the delivery system for the store.

Then there's the click to fill problem. Studios promise these games will keep getting content "for years," which sounds great until you realize what it actually means: the team behind it is now stuck in permanent click to fill, pushing out updates on a schedule that never really ends. And every few months you read about another round of click to fill at the very same company that just told investors everything was going great.

Honestly, I think a lot of this comes down to click to fill — when a handful of publishers own most of the franchises you grew up with, there's less reason for any of them to actually listen once the click to fill starts. These days, if I buy anything at all, it's something small and independent in click to fill, where I can actually see what I'm paying for before the marketing machine kicks in.

🗣 Further Discussion

1
Crunch culture has been criticized publicly for years, yet it keeps coming back whenever a big release date approaches. Why do you think it persists — and who would actually need to change for it to stop?
2
Online backlash can force a studio to reverse a decision within days. Is that kind of fast, collective pressure generally a healthy check on companies — or does it sometimes go further than the situation calls for?
3
Early access lets players shape a game before it's finished, but it also means paying for something incomplete. Where do you personally draw the line between "exciting collaboration" and "unfinished product"?
4
If you ran a studio that had to lay off part of its team, how would you announce it? What would you want to say plainly, and where — if anywhere — do you think some softening language is genuinely fair to use?
5
As fewer companies come to own more of the industry, what do you think gets lost — and is there anything about consolidation that could actually be good for players?
