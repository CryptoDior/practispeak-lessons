Vocabulary
8 words for talking about how creators build, hold, and cash in on an audience
01
engagement
NOUN · HOW ACTIVELY AN AUDIENCE INTERACTS WITH SOMETHING — COMMENTS, REACTIONS, WATCH TIME
often paired with: drive engagement, an engagement spike, low engagement
It's the difference between people simply seeing your content and people actually doing something with it — and it's the number every platform quietly optimizes for.
EXAMPLE
"The algorithm doesn't really care if people loved the stream — it cares whether they engaged with it, and for how long."
02
persona
NOUN · THE PUBLIC CHARACTER YOU PERFORM ON CAMERA, WHICH MAY OR MAY NOT MATCH WHO YOU ARE OFF IT
often paired with: build a persona, an on-stream persona, drop the persona
It's the version of you that exists specifically for an audience — sometimes close to the real thing, sometimes a much louder, faster, funnier exaggeration of it.
EXAMPLE
"Off camera, she's quiet and a little awkward — her on-stream persona is loud, fast-talking, and never runs out of things to say."
03
authenticity
NOUN · THE SENSE THAT SOMEONE IS BEING GENUINE RATHER THAN SIMPLY PERFORMING FOR AN AUDIENCE
often paired with: question someone's authenticity, feel authentic, a sense of authenticity
It's the quality audiences are constantly trying to detect — and the one creators are constantly, sometimes a little too visibly, trying to project.
EXAMPLE
"Audiences can usually tell the difference between authenticity and a performance of authenticity — and they tend to reward the real thing, eventually."
04
hook
NOUN · AN OPENING MOMENT DESIGNED TO GRAB ATTENTION BEFORE SOMEONE SCROLLS PAST
often paired with: land a hook, open with a hook, a strong hook
You don't get long to convince someone to keep watching — a hook is the few seconds of work that buys you the rest of the video.
EXAMPLE
"You've got about three seconds to land a hook before most people swipe away to the next thing."
05
callout
NOUN · A PUBLIC STATEMENT NAMING AND CRITICIZING SOMEONE, USUALLY TO AN AUDIENCE THAT'S ALREADY WATCHING
often paired with: post a callout, a callout video, the callout blew up
It's a public accusation made in front of an audience rather than in private — which tends to spread fast and leave very little room to respond calmly.
EXAMPLE
"The callout went up at midnight and had two hundred thousand views by morning — long before anyone involved had a chance to respond."
06
clout
NOUN (INFORMAL) · INFLUENCE OR STATUS, ESPECIALLY THE KIND THAT COMES FROM BEING WIDELY KNOWN ONLINE
often paired with: chase clout, clout-chasing, gain clout
It's social currency — the sense that being seen, talked about, or argued with online is worth something in itself, regardless of whether the attention is positive.
EXAMPLE
"Half the arguments in that community aren't really about the issue — they're about who gets to walk away with more clout."
07
pivot
VERB / NOUN · TO SUDDENLY CHANGE DIRECTION OR STRATEGY, OFTEN IN RESPONSE TO WHAT IS OR ISN'T WORKING
often paired with: pivot to, a content pivot, pivot the channel
It's the moment a creator stops doing the thing that used to work and starts doing something else entirely — sometimes by choice, sometimes because the numbers left them no other option.
EXAMPLE
"When the gaming content stopped performing, the whole channel pivoted to cooking videos almost overnight — and somehow it worked."
08
monetize
VERB · TO TURN AN AUDIENCE, A SKILL, OR A PIECE OF CONTENT INTO A SOURCE OF INCOME
often paired with: monetize a channel, ways to monetize, monetization eligibility
It's the step where an audience stops being just an audience and starts being, at least partly, a source of income — through ads, subscriptions, sponsorships, or merch.
EXAMPLE
"It took her two years of streaming for free before she had enough of an audience to seriously think about monetizing it."
Language Focus
8 rhetorical devices creators reach for — and what they sound like in the wild
01
rhetorical question
A QUESTION ASKED FOR EFFECT RATHER THAN TO GET AN ANSWER — IT INVITES THE LISTENER TO AGREE BEFORE THEY'VE REALLY THOUGHT ABOUT IT
often built with: "Who doesn't...", "Haven't you ever...", "Why do we...?"
It does the listener's thinking for them — by the time they register it as a question, they've usually already nodded along to the answer it implies.
EXAMPLE
"Who doesn't want to feel like they're part of something bigger than themselves?"
02
tricolon (rule of three)
GROUPING THREE WORDS, PHRASES, OR IDEAS TOGETHER — SOMETHING ABOUT THREES JUST SOUNDS COMPLETE TO THE EAR
often built with: verb, verb, and verb / "no X, no Y, just Z"
Two items sound like a list; three sound like a rule — which is exactly why slogans, mottos, and stream taglines lean on this shape so heavily.
EXAMPLE
"Show up, speak up, and never apologize for taking up space."
03
anaphora
REPEATING THE SAME WORD OR PHRASE AT THE START OF SUCCESSIVE CLAUSES, BUILDING MOMENTUM WITH EACH REPETITION
often built with: "We didn't have... We didn't have... We didn't even have..."
It turns a list of plain facts into something closer to a drumbeat — each repetition raises the emotional stakes a little before the final clause breaks the pattern and lands the point.
EXAMPLE
"We didn't have a studio. We didn't have a budget. We didn't even have a plan — we just had an audience who showed up anyway."
04
hyperbole
DELIBERATE, OBVIOUS EXAGGERATION — NOBODY IS SUPPOSED TO TAKE IT LITERALLY, BUT EVERYONE IS SUPPOSED TO FEEL THE ENTHUSIASM BEHIND IT
often built with: "the single greatest...", "literally changed my life", "best thing ever"
It trades precision for impact — and in a feed full of careful, hedged claims, a confident exaggeration is often the thing that actually gets noticed.
EXAMPLE
"This is, without a doubt, the single greatest community on the entire internet."
05
direct address
SPEAKING TO ONE IMAGINED VIEWER USING "YOU," AS IF THE WHOLE AUDIENCE ISN'T WATCHING AT THE SAME TIME
often built with: "you — yes, you...", "if you're watching this..."
A crowd doesn't feel singled out, but a "you" does — which is exactly why creators reach for it the moment they want a stranger to feel personally chosen.
EXAMPLE
"You — yes, you, scrolling past at one in the morning — you're exactly the person this was made for."
06
ethos (appeal to credibility)
PERSUADING BY ESTABLISHING THAT YOU'RE QUALIFIED, EXPERIENCED, OR TRUSTWORTHY ENOUGH TO BE BELIEVED
often built with: "I've been doing this for...", "after X years of...", "speaking from experience..."
Before anyone accepts your conclusion, they usually want a reason to trust the person making it — and a quick résumé of survived setbacks does that more efficiently than any argument could.
EXAMPLE
"I've been doing this for six years, through three platform changes and two algorithm collapses, so when I say this works, I mean it."
07
pathos (appeal to emotion)
PERSUADING BY MAKING SOMEONE FEEL SOMETHING — SYMPATHY, NOSTALGIA, HOPE — RATHER THAN BY ARGUING A POINT OUTRIGHT
often built with: "I think about the person who...", "imagine how it feels to..."
A feeling, once it lands, tends to do the convincing that an argument never quite manages — which is why so many appeals skip the logic and go straight for the heart.
EXAMPLE
"I think about the kid watching this alone in their room, feeling like nobody understands them — and that's exactly who I'm doing this for."
08
strategic self-deprecation
PUTTING YOURSELF DOWN ON PURPOSE, IN A WAY CALCULATED TO MAKE YOU SEEM MORE LIKEABLE, RELATABLE, OR LESS THREATENING
often built with: "I'm not even that good at...", "honestly, I have no idea what I'm doing"
A small, well-placed admission of weakness often does more for someone's likability than any boast could — it invites the audience to feel like they're laughing along, not being sold to.
EXAMPLE
"I'm not even that good at the game, honestly — I think people just like watching me lose with a smile on my face."
In Use
See the same rhetorical moves shift register — from a media-studies lecture, to a creator panel, to a comments section at midnight
Lecture / Media StudiesOn the Architecture of Online Persuasion

"Contemporary streaming culture runs on a small, recognizable set of rhetorical moves, repeated so often that audiences barely register them as persuasion at all. A creator opens with a hook calculated to survive the first three seconds of a scroll, builds momentum through anaphora and the rule of three, then closes the loop with a direct appeal to the very audience it has just spent several minutes constructing. None of this makes the appeal insincere — but it does mean that what looks like spontaneous connection is, more often than not, a carefully rehearsed structure wearing the costume of authenticity."

Notice the cool, observational distance here — "runs on," "is, more often than not" — the lecture register names the technique without ever stepping into it. The goal is to describe persuasion, not perform it.
Creator PanelPanel — "Charisma Isn't Magic, It's a Toolkit"

"Honestly, the biggest thing people get wrong is thinking charisma is something you either have or you don't. It's not — it's a set of tools, and you can absolutely learn them. Repetition in threes. Speaking directly to one imagined viewer instead of a faceless crowd. Telling a true story about yourself before you ever ask anyone for anything — that's just ethos, building trust before you make the ask. None of that is manipulation, in my opinion — it's just knowing how to be heard in a room full of people shouting."

This speaker names the techniques almost like a recipe — "repetition in threes," "speaking directly to one imagined viewer" — confident, conversational, a little defensive about the word "manipulation" before anyone's even used it.
Comments SectionThread — "the 'i almost didn't post this' bit, every single time"

"ok but has anyone else noticed every single streamer says some version of 'i almost didn't post this' right before the thing that obviously took them three weeks to plan?? like we see you. the 'i'm just a small creator' bit hits different when you've got 400k followers lol. not even mad about it, it clearly works, just funny how predictable it's gotten"

Compare this to the panel above — same underlying move (strategic self-deprecation, manufactured spontaneity) but named here with a wink rather than a label. The audience has learned the trick well enough to perform their own commentary on it.
Register & Stance
How persuasive language shifts shape across contexts — from the careful hedge of a lecture to the bluntness of a comment thread
ContextTypical StanceExample
Media studies lecture
Formal, analytical, observational		 Highly formal "The appeal functions, in effect, as a carefully rehearsed structure wearing the costume of spontaneity."
Industry interview / press
Reflective, semi-formal account		 Highly formal "We found that opening on a direct question consistently held attention longer than opening on a statement."
Creator panel / podcast
Conversational, confident, a little defensive		 Semi-formal "It's not magic — it's a toolkit, and honestly, anyone can learn to use it."
Stream chat / live commentary
Informal, immediate, performative		 Semi-formal "okay THAT was the hook of the century, no notes"
Comments / forum thread
Informal, blunt, often a little mocking		 Informal / emotional "the 'i almost didn't post this' bit again?? we SEE you bro"
✅Naming a rhetorical device isn't the same as condemning it — a lecture can describe a hook with total neutrality, while a comment thread might call the exact same move "manipulative" in the very next sentence. The device is constant; the stance toward it is what shifts.
💡Watch how creators often name their own techniques out loud — "that's just ethos, basically" — which does two things at once: it signals media literacy and quietly defends the move from criticism before anyone else can raise it.
⚠️"Authentic" and "performed" aren't opposites in this world — they're often the same thing, viewed from different distances. A lecture register tends to hold both possibilities open at once; a comment thread usually picks one and runs with it.
❌Don't mistake a confident tone for a strong argument. Hyperbole and direct address can make a claim feel true long before anyone has actually checked whether it is — which is precisely the gap careful listening is meant to close.

How I Actually Grew This Channel (the Boring Truth)
A short creator blog post — fill in the blanks with words from the bank above, then check your answers

For the first year, almost nobody watched. I obsessed over the click to fill — the first three seconds — convinced that was the only thing standing between me and an audience. It helped a little, but not nearly as much as I thought it would.

What actually changed things was a quiet click to fill. I'd been making generic gaming clips because that's what everyone else was doing, and one day I just stopped, and started making videos about the weird side conversations that happen mid-game instead. That's when click to fill really started climbing — not views exactly, more like comments, the kind where people actually talk to each other in the replies.

I also had to work out what my on-camera click to fill even was. I'm louder and faster on stream than I am in real life, and for a long time that felt fake. Eventually I realized the gap mattered less than I'd worried — what mattered more was click to fill, and that's not quite the same thing as never performing. It's more about whether the performance still feels like you underneath it.

I went through one ugly click to fill early on, too — somebody made a video about something I'd said two years before, and for about a week it felt like the whole internet was talking about me, for all the wrong reasons. Good lesson in how fast click to fill can turn into something you never actually asked for. These days the channel can finally click to fill — sponsorships mostly, plus a small membership thing — but that took about three years of doing it for free first.

🗣 Further Discussion

1
Creators often name their own techniques out loud — "that's just ethos, basically." Does saying the quiet part out loud make the technique feel more honest, or is it just a cleverer version of the same persuasion?
2
Is there a meaningful difference between a politician using anaphora in a speech and a streamer using it in a sponsored segment? Or is rhetoric just rhetoric, regardless of who's reaching for it?
3
"I almost didn't post this" has become such a recognizable line that audiences openly joke about it — and creators keep using it anyway. What does it mean for a persuasive technique to keep working even after everyone can see it coming?
4
Strategic self-deprecation only works if it doesn't look strategic. Can you think of an example — from streaming or anywhere else — where you could feel the gears turning behind someone's "aw, it's nothing"?
5
Pick a creator you actually enjoy watching. Which of the devices from this lesson do they lean on most — and does naming the technique change how it feels to watch them use it?
