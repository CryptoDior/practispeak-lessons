Vocabulary
8 terms for discussing gaming culture, identity, and representation at an advanced level
0 1
IDENTITY
Noun

Collocations: cultural identity · collective identity · identity formation · sense of identity · contested identity

Who you are — the mix of background, beliefs, and groups that make you, you. It's not one fixed thing; it changes as you grow and meet new people. Ex: Being a gamer might be a big part of someone's identity, the same way being a musician is for someone else.

Example

"Gaming's a huge part of my identity now — it's not just something I do for fun, it's kind of who I am."

0 2
SUBCULTURE
Noun

Collocations: gaming subculture · subculture formation · countercultural · mainstream vs subculture · subcultural identity

A smaller group inside a bigger society that has its own style, habits, and way of talking — often a bit different from the "mainstream." Ex: Gamers used to be seen as their own subculture, with slang and inside jokes that outsiders just didn't get.

Example

"Gaming used to be this little subculture — just a bunch of people in arcades and basements. Now it's everywhere."

0 3
DISCOURSE
Noun

Collocations: cultural discourse · public discourse · dominant discourse · shift in discourse · discourse around X

The way people talk and write about a topic — the opinions and attitudes that keep coming up whenever it's discussed. Ex: Twenty years ago, the discourse around gaming was mostly negative — today people are more likely to talk about it as a real art form.

Example

"The discourse around gaming has really changed — people used to panic about it, but now they actually talk about it as art and culture."

0 4
STIGMA
Noun

Collocations: social stigma · attached/associated stigma · carry a stigma · stigmatised · erode/challenge stigma

A negative label that society attaches to something, making people feel ashamed of it — even when it's not actually harmful. Ex: There used to be a stigma around being a "gamer" — people assumed it meant you were lazy or had no friends.

Example

"There's still a bit of stigma around gaming — like people assuming it's childish or a waste of time — but it's way less than it used to be."

0 5
REPRESENTATION
Noun

Collocations: media representation · diverse representation · question of representation · under-representation · misrepresentation

How a group of people is shown in movies, games, or the news — and whether that picture feels accurate and fair. Ex: Fans keep asking for better representation in games — more characters who actually look and feel like them.

Example

"People care a lot more about representation in games now — like who the main characters are and whose stories actually get told."

0 6
LEGITIMACY
Noun

Collocations: cultural legitimacy · claim legitimacy · legitimacy of X · afford/deny legitimacy · contested legitimacy

Being taken seriously and accepted as "real" — instead of being dismissed as silly or unimportant. Ex: Esports has slowly earned legitimacy — it's now shown on TV and treated like a real sport.

Example

"Nobody really questions gaming's legitimacy as an art form anymore — the bigger question is who gets to decide what counts as 'good.'"

0 7
COMMODIFY
Verb

Collocations: commodify culture · commodification of X · commodified identity · commercial commodification · resist commodification

To turn something — a culture, a style, an idea — into a product that companies can sell for profit. Ex: Some fans feel brands have commodified gaming culture — slapping gamer logos on T-shirts just to make money.

Example

"Some people think big companies have commodified gaming culture — basically slapping a logo on it and selling it back to us."

0 8
NUANCE
Noun

Collocations: subtle nuance · lack of nuance · require/demand nuance · nuanced argument · fail to capture nuance

A small but important difference in meaning or detail — the kind of thing you miss if you only look at the big picture. Ex: Saying "all gamers are the same" misses the nuance — a casual mobile player and a pro esports athlete live very different lives.

Example

"You really need some nuance when you talk about gaming culture — a pro esports player and someone who plays Candy Crush on the bus live pretty different lives."

Language Focus
8 hedging and discourse devices for seminar discussion and academic writing
0 1
"It could be argued that…"
Classic Academic Hedge

A soft way to give an opinion. Instead of saying "this is true," you say "here's one way to look at it" — which leaves room for other views.

Example

"It could be argued that gaming becoming so popular hasn't really answered the identity question — it's just changed what we're arguing about."

0 2
"There is a tendency for…"
Hedged Generalisation

Describes a pattern carefully, without claiming it always happens. Safer (and more accurate) than saying "everyone" or "always."

Example

"There is a tendency for big brands to commodify gaming culture — turning what used to feel personal into something they can just sell."

0 3
"To some extent, this suggests…"
Partial Claim

Says something is partly true. You agree with an idea, but not 100% — it shows you've thought about its limits, not just the headline.

Example

"To some extent, this suggests that the stigma around gaming hasn't disappeared — it's just moved onto certain types of games or certain communities."

0 4
"One might question whether…"
Polite Academic Challenge

A polite way to challenge an idea. Instead of saying "that's wrong," you raise a doubt and let the listener think it through for themselves.

Example

"One might question whether calling yourself a 'gamer' even means as much these days, since pretty much everyone plays games now."

0 5
"The evidence would seem to indicate…"
Cautious Claim

A careful way to say "the facts point this way" — but with a touch of doubt added, because the evidence isn't 100% certain yet.

Example

"The evidence would seem to indicate that younger gamers feel a lot more relaxed about their gamer identity than older generations did."

0 6
"This raises the question of…"
Discussion Opener

Introduces a bigger question without trying to answer it right away — a good way to keep a discussion open instead of shutting it down.

Example

"This raises the question of whether gaming can still feel like its own thing now that pretty much everyone's doing it."

0 7
"While it is true that…, one must also consider…"
Concession + Counter-Argument

Agree with one side first, then add your own point. It shows you can see both sides of an argument before landing on yours — which makes your point land harder.

Example

"While it is true that gaming is more visible than ever, one must also consider how much of that is just companies chasing money rather than caring about the culture."

0 8
"I would tentatively suggest that…"
Personal Position with Epistemic Humility

A gentle way to share your own idea. "Tentatively" tells people you're open to being wrong or hearing a different opinion — not announcing the final word.

Example

"I would tentatively suggest that the real debate isn't whether gaming matters anymore — it's about where the culture goes from here."

💡 Language Focus — Hedging as a Pragmatic Tool

Hedging isn't about sounding unsure — it's about being precise. Saying "it could be argued" instead of "this is true" shows you've actually thought about the topic, and it leaves room for other people to see it differently. That's a skill worth having, in a seminar or just in everyday conversation.

Modal hedges

Form: might / could / would + verb
Signal: possibility, not certainty
"This could represent a shift."
The softest hedge — appropriate when evidence is suggestive but not conclusive.

Adverbial hedges

Form: arguably / seemingly / tentatively / apparently
Signal: the claim is debatable or provisional
"Gaming has arguably become…"
Single-word hedges that modify the entire claim — efficient and widely used in academic prose.

Distancing phrases

Form: it could be argued / one might suggest / there is a tendency
Signal: this is one reading among several
"It could be argued that…"
The most powerful hedges — they step back from the claim entirely, inviting the reader to evaluate rather than simply accept.

Hedging in context — same claim, different levels of epistemic commitment

"Gaming is the defining cultural phenomenon of the 21st century." → unhedged — likely to be dismissed as overstatement
"Gaming has arguably become one of the defining cultural phenomena of the 21st century." → adverbial hedge — more credible, still clear
"It could be argued that gaming represents one of the defining cultural formations of the early 21st century." → distancing phrase — invites engagement rather than demanding agreement
"While it is true that gaming has achieved cultural prominence, one must also consider whether this prominence has been accompanied by genuine critical engagement." → concession + counter — shows sophisticated handling of complexity
⚠️ Over-hedging undermines rather than strengthens. "It might possibly be tentatively suggested that gaming could perhaps be considered somewhat significant" — the accumulated hedges leave nothing behind. Each sentence needs one primary hedging device, deployed for a clear purpose. Hedging is a precision instrument, not a defensive shield.
In Use
See how the same words and hedging phrases sound in a blog post, a podcast chat, and a heated online thread
Blog / Opinion Piece"Gaming Went Mainstream — Did It Lose Itself?"

"It could be argued that gaming's move from the margins to the mainstream is one of the biggest culture shifts of our time — and one we barely stop to think about. There is a tendency to tell this story as a simple win: an outsider group finally gets the respect it deserved. To some extent, that's true — gaming's legitimacy as an art form is barely questioned anymore. But something else is going on too. As gaming gets commodified — turned into fashion lines, ad campaigns, branded merch — the question of identity gets messier. I would tentatively suggest that the real challenge facing gaming culture today isn't outside stigma — it's holding onto any shared identity at all, now that billions of people play."

Notice how this writer softens almost every claim — "it could be argued," "there is a tendency," "I would tentatively suggest" — instead of just stating things as flat facts. Also notice how many lesson words show up close together (legitimacy, commodified, identity, stigma) — that's normal when someone's taking a topic seriously, even in everyday writing.
Panel DiscussionPodcast — "Is Gaming a Cultural Identity?"

"I think what's interesting — and I would tentatively suggest people don't talk about this enough — is that there's never really been just one 'gamer identity.' There is a tendency, especially in the news, to talk about gamers like they're all the same. But the discourse inside gaming communities tells a very different story. To some extent, all the talk about whether gaming 'counts' as legitimate has actually distracted from a bigger question: representation. Whose stories get told? Who gets to be the hero? This raises the question of whether gaming culture is really growing — or just getting repackaged and sold as one narrow version of itself, which is its own kind of commodification."

This speaker sounds more relaxed than the blog post above — they say "I think" — but they're still reaching for the same advanced vocab and hedging phrases. Notice how they end with "this raises the question of…" — a handy way to bring up a new idea without trying to answer it on the spot.
Public / Social MediaThread — Direct, Minimal Hedging

"Gaming culture didn't become legitimate. It got bought. The subculture had its style ripped out, repackaged, and sold back to us by the same people who spent years calling it a waste of time. What we're calling 'legitimacy' is just commodification with better PR. The stigma didn't go away — it just moved onto whoever's inconvenient for the brand right now. And most people are too busy celebrating to notice what got lost."

Same basic opinion as the blog post above — just said with zero hedging. Compare "Gaming culture didn't become legitimate. It got bought." with the much softer version up top. Neither way of saying it is "wrong" — it just depends on where you are: a heated online debate calls for directness, a seminar calls for hedging.
Register & Stance
How hedging level and vocabulary shift across contexts — and what that signals about your position
ContextHedging LevelExample
Academic essay
Formal written analysis		 High — essential "It could be argued that gaming going mainstream hasn't solved the identity question — it's just changed it."
Seminar contribution
Spoken, collaborative		 High — shows respect "One might question whether splitting gaming into 'subculture' vs. 'mainstream' even makes sense anymore."
Panel / podcast discussion
Semi-formal, discursive		 Moderate — conversational "I would tentatively suggest that the bigger question now isn't legitimacy — it's representation."
Journalistic feature
Neutral, accessible		 Low-moderate "Gaming's so-called 'identity crisis' isn't really about being accepted anymore — it's about staying united."
Social media / public debate
Informal, direct		 Minimal / None "Gaming culture didn't become legitimate. It got bought."
✅Hedging makes you sound thoughtful, not unsure. The people who do it best are the ones who know exactly how confident they really are about something — and say so honestly.
💡Hedging invites dialogue. An unhedged claim like "gaming is the most important cultural form of our era" demands agreement or confrontation. "It could be argued that gaming represents one of the defining cultural formations of the 21st century" opens a conversation.
⚠️Over-hedging is a real problem at C1-C2 level. "It might possibly be tentatively suggested that gaming could perhaps be considered somewhat culturally significant" — the accumulated hedges leave nothing meaningful behind. One well-chosen device per claim is enough.
❌Don't confuse hedging with vagueness. Hedging is precise and intentional — you choose a specific device for a specific reason. Vagueness is simply being unclear about what you mean. "Gaming is kind of important in a way" is vague, not hedged.

🗣 Further Discussion

1
Some people say gaming has "made it" — it's mainstream now, it's respected, it's everywhere. Do you think that means the old questions about gaming identity are settled? Or has something just changed shape rather than disappeared?
2
Hedging makes you sound thoughtful in a seminar — but the same kind of hedging can sound weak or wishy-washy in an online argument. Why do you think the same language works so differently depending on where you are?
3
The blog post in Blog Corner says we're "losing some nuance" when we lump all gamers together. Is that always a bad thing — or can painting with a broad brush sometimes be useful, even if it's not 100% accurate?
4
If big companies have commodified gaming culture — turning it into hoodies, energy drinks, and gamer chairs — does it still feel like "their" culture to long-time gamers? Or does something change once it becomes a product?
5
Think about your own relationship with gaming. Using the vocabulary and hedging phrases from this lesson, give a 3-minute talk about where you stand: is gaming a big part of your identity, or just something you do sometimes?
