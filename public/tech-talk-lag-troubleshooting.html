Vocabulary
8 words for talking about tech problems — and the causes behind them
0 1
LAG
Noun / Verb

A delay between your input and what happens on screen — the most common and most frustrating tech problem in gaming. To lag is to experience this delay.

Example

"The lag was so bad I couldn't even move — my character was frozen on screen while the game continued without me."

0 2
PING
Noun

The time it takes for data to travel from your device to the game server and back — measured in milliseconds. Low ping is good. High ping causes lag.

Example

"My ping jumped to 400 milliseconds and I instantly started rubber-banding across the map — completely unplayable."

0 3
PACKET LOSS
Noun

When chunks of data (packets) fail to reach their destination — causing stuttering, rubber-banding, and sudden disconnections. Often worse than high ping.

Example

"Packet loss was the real problem — every few seconds a chunk of data disappeared and my character stuttered across the map."

0 4
BANDWIDTH
Noun

How much data your connection can carry at once — like the width of a pipe. When bandwidth is maxed out, everything slows down.

Example

"My bandwidth was maxed out because my roommate was streaming 4K video — that's what was causing all the lag."

0 5
LATENCY
Noun

The technical term for delay in a network connection — the time between an action and its response. High latency = lag. Used more formally than "ping" or "lag."

Example

"High latency in a first-person shooter is a death sentence — by the time your shot registers, the enemy has already moved."

0 6
BOTTLENECK
Noun

A single slow component that limits everything else's performance — even if the rest of your setup is fast, the bottleneck brings it all down.

Example

"The bottleneck wasn't my internet — it was my old router on the floor. Everything else was fast, but the router killed the connection."

0 7
THROTTLE
Verb

To deliberately limit speed or performance — ISPs throttle gaming traffic during peak hours; routers throttle themselves when overheating to prevent damage.

Example

"My ISP was throttling my connection during peak hours — that's why my ping was fine in the morning but awful every evening."

0 8
TROUBLESHOOT
Verb

To identify and fix problems systematically — testing one possible cause at a time until you find what's actually wrong. The opposite of guessing.

Example

"I spent two hours troubleshooting before I found it — a single background app was using all my upload bandwidth."

Phrases
8 cause/effect phrases for diagnosing and explaining tech problems
0 1
"My connection keeps dropping because…"
Direct Cause — because

"Because" introduces a cause directly — everything after it explains why something happened. The simplest and most natural way to state a cause in English.

Example

"My connection keeps dropping because my router is overheating — it throttles itself and loses signal every twenty minutes."

0 2
"That's what's causing the lag."
Naming the Cause

The most satisfying sentence in any troubleshooting conversation — it signals you've found the answer. Use it only when you've actually identified the source of the problem.

Example

"You've got six devices on the same WiFi — that's what's causing the lag. Plug in with ethernet and it'll disappear."

0 3
"High ping leads to rubber-banding."
Cause → Effect (leads to)

"Leads to" moves from a cause directly to its consequence — it's slightly more formal than "causes" and works well for explaining chains of technical problems.

Example

"High ping leads to rubber-banding — your client thinks you're in one position, but the server says you're somewhere completely different."

0 4
"It could be down to your ISP throttling you."
Casual Attribution

"Down to" casually attributes blame or cause to something — it's British English that's spread through gaming culture. "It's down to X" means "X is responsible for this."

Example

"Your speeds look fine at 9am but tank at 8pm every night — it could be down to your ISP throttling you during peak hours."

0 5
"The lag spikes whenever someone else uses the WiFi."
Conditional Pattern — whenever

"Whenever" identifies a repeated trigger — every time X happens, Y follows. It's one of the most useful patterns for explaining intermittent tech problems.

Example

"The lag spikes whenever someone else uses the WiFi — that's a bandwidth problem, not a server problem."

0 6
"As a result, I'm getting packet loss."
Effect Language — as a result

"As a result" introduces a consequence — everything before it was the cause, everything after is the effect. More formal than "so" and clearer than "then."

Example

"My router is too far from my room, and as a result I'm getting packet loss every few minutes — the signal just isn't strong enough."

0 7
"It's probably your bandwidth — try closing background apps."
Diagnosis + Fix

The natural structure of a troubleshooting suggestion: state the likely cause, then immediately offer a fix. "Probably" keeps it honest — you're diagnosing, not guaranteeing.

Example

"It's probably your bandwidth — try closing background apps and check if anything is downloading in the background."

0 8
"Ever since I switched servers, my ping dropped significantly."
Temporal Cause/Effect — ever since

"Ever since" marks a point in the past where a change happened — and confirms the effect has continued from that moment to now. It shows cause and ongoing consequence at once.

Example

"Ever since I switched to the EU servers, my ping dropped from 180 to 28 — I should have done it months ago."

💡 Grammar Focus — Cause/Effect Language

Every tech problem has a cause and an effect. English has three ways to express this relationship — knowing which to use makes the difference between sounding vague and sounding precise.

Direct — cause moves to effect

Pattern: X causes / leads to / results in Y
Signal: you state the cause, then the consequence
"High ping leads to rubber-banding."
Use when you can clearly name both the cause and the effect in one sentence.

Attribution — naming what's responsible

Pattern: Y is caused by / stems from / is down to X
Signal: you start with the effect, then reveal the cause
"It could be down to your ISP."
Use when diagnosing — you know the effect (lag) and are pointing to the likely cause.

Conditional — trigger and consequence

Pattern: Whenever / If / Ever since X, Y
Signal: a recurring trigger or a moment of change
"Whenever someone else uses WiFi, it lags."
Use for intermittent or repeating problems — "whenever" identifies the pattern, "ever since" marks the turning point.

Gaming tech — cause/effect chains in action

"My ISP is throttling my connection during peak hours, which leads to high latency, which results in rubber-banding." → full chain: cause → effect → effect
"Whenever I'm on WiFi, my packet loss goes up — as a result, I disconnect every few rounds." → conditional trigger + result
"The bottleneck was my old router. That's what was causing all the lag — not the server, not the game." → attribution after investigation
"Ever since I switched to ethernet, my ping dropped from 120 to 18 and I've had zero packet loss." → temporal cause with ongoing effect
"It could be down to bandwidth — six devices streaming will max out any router." → casual diagnosis with explanation
⚠️ Watch "because" vs "because of": "because" introduces a full clause — "I'm lagging because my bandwidth is maxed out." "Because of" introduces a noun or noun phrase — "I'm lagging because of my bandwidth." You cannot say "I'm lagging because of my bandwidth is maxed out" — that mixes both forms incorrectly.
In Action
How cause/effect language sounds across different gaming tech contexts
Discord VoiceDiagnosing Lag Mid-Game

"Bro, are you rubber-banding? You just teleported across the map. — Yeah sorry, my connection keeps dropping because my flatmate started streaming something on Netflix. He's killing all the bandwidth. — That's what's causing the lag then. Tell him to pause it or use ethernet. — I've got three other devices on the same router too. — Okay that's definitely it. Whenever you overload the WiFi like that, everyone's ping goes up. As a result you're basically unplayable right now. Plug in with ethernet and let's troubleshoot this properly."

Notice how the conversation moves from observation ("you're rubber-banding") to cause identification ("that's what's causing the lag") to pattern explanation ("whenever you overload the WiFi") to consequence ("as a result you're unplayable") to solution. This is the natural structure of any tech troubleshooting conversation.
Tech ForumStructured Troubleshooting Post

"Post: High ping every evening — suspected ISP throttling. I play on wired ethernet with a 200 megabit plan. My ping in the morning is consistently 18 to 22 milliseconds. However, every evening between 7pm and 11pm, it spikes to 150 to 200 milliseconds, leading to rubber-banding and occasional disconnects. The issue stems from somewhere between my router and the game servers — my local bandwidth tests show full speed, so the bottleneck is not in my home network. It could be down to my ISP throttling gaming traffic during peak hours. As a result, ranked play in the evening is close to impossible. Has anyone successfully troubleshot this and confirmed ISP throttling?"

Forum posts use more formal cause/effect language than voice chat — "the issue stems from," "leading to," and "as a result" all appear here. Notice how the writer systematically eliminates possible causes (local bandwidth is fine) before pointing to the likely culprit (ISP throttling). This is structured troubleshooting in writing.
Live StreamChat Diagnosing the Streamer's Lag

"The streamer is confused — they are on wired ethernet, their download speed is 500 megabits, and yet the game is stuttering every few seconds. Chat has already figured it out. Several viewers point out that streaming and gaming simultaneously splits the upload bandwidth — as a result, the game server is not receiving data fast enough. Others suggest the lag could be down to ISP throttling, which becomes more aggressive during peak evening hours. One viewer explains it clearly: whenever you are encoding a live stream and playing a competitive game at the same time, your upload is the bottleneck. The streamer closes the stream encoding software, tests the ping — it drops from 180 milliseconds to 24. That is what was causing the lag. A single process, using all the upload bandwidth."

Stream chat troubleshooting is collaborative and fast — multiple viewers offer different hypotheses simultaneously. Notice that the cause/effect language still appears naturally, even in this casual context. "That is what was causing the lag" lands as a satisfying conclusion regardless of register.

✅"Leads to," "stems from," and "as a result" work across every register — from casual Discord chat to formal support tickets. They're the most versatile cause/effect phrases in the lesson.
💡"That's what's causing the lag" is register-neutral — it sounds natural in a Discord voice call and equally satisfying in a written post-mortem. Use it whenever you've confirmed the source of a problem.
⚠️Watch "because" vs "because of" — "because" needs a full clause after it ("because my bandwidth is maxed out"), while "because of" needs a noun or phrase ("because of high latency"). Mixing them is one of the most common errors at B1-B2 level.
❌Don't use "due to" as a standalone opener in informal contexts — "Due to lag, I lost" is technically correct but sounds overly formal for Discord. In casual gaming, use "because of" or "it's down to" instead.
In Context
Read the story — highlighted phrases are from this lesson
Why I Lag and What I Did About It

For six months I blamed my ISP. Every evening at around 8pm, my ping would spike from 25 to 180 milliseconds, leading to rubber-banding and dropped connections in ranked matches. I was convinced the problem was out of my control.

Then I actually started to troubleshoot.

The first thing I checked was my bandwidth. I had five devices connected to the same WiFi at the time — two phones, a laptop, a smart TV that was auto-updating, and my gaming PC. As a result, my connection was being split five ways during peak evening hours. That was part of it, but not all of it.

The real discovery was the router itself. It was three years old, positioned on the floor behind a sofa, and it was overheating every evening after hours of use. Overheating causes routers to throttle their own performance — the device deliberately slows down to protect itself. That's what was causing the lag. Not my ISP. Not the game servers. My three-year-old router on the floor.

I moved the router, plugged in with ethernet, and closed every background app. Ever since I made those changes, my ping has been stable at 22 milliseconds. The lag is gone.

It could have been down to a hundred different things. But the fix took twenty minutes once I actually looked for the cause.

Think about why each cause/effect phrase was chosen — not just what it means.

A
They happen independently with no connection between them
B
High ping is the cause and rubber-banding is the direct effect — "leads to" always moves from cause to consequence
C
Rubber-banding is what causes the high ping
D
They are two words that mean the same thing
2. The narrator says "That's what was causing the lag." What had they actually discovered?
A
Their ISP was throttling the connection during peak hours
B
They had too many devices on WiFi
C
Their overheating router was throttling its own performance to protect itself from damage
D
Their bandwidth plan was too small for gaming
3. "As a result, my connection was being split five ways." What is the cause and what is the effect?
A
Effect: five devices connected. Cause: the split connection — the split caused the devices to connect
B
Cause: five devices connected. Effect: the connection being split — "as a result" always introduces the consequence
C
Both the devices and the split connection are causes of the same problem
D
"As a result" signals uncertainty — the narrator is not sure what caused the split
4. "Ever since I made those changes, my ping has been stable." What does "ever since" tell us about time?
A
The improvement started at a specific moment in the past and has continued without interruption to the present
B
The changes were temporary and the ping might get worse again
C
The changes happened very recently — "ever since" implies something just happened
D
The narrator regrets not making the changes earlier
Speaking
Choose a scenario — diagnose, explain, or debate
Pick one option. You have 3–4 minutes to prepare and speak. Use at least 4 vocabulary words and 4 cause/effect phrases from the lesson. The goal is to sound like someone who actually understands the problem — not just someone who knows the words.
🔎Option A

Tech Support

One player describes a lag problem in detail. The other asks follow-up questions to diagnose the cause. Use cause/effect language throughout — "whenever," "that's what's causing it," "it could be down to." Then swap roles.

⛓️Option B

The Chain Reaction

Describe a tech problem where one cause led to multiple effects — ISP throttling → high latency → rubber-banding → disconnects. Use at least 5 different cause/effect phrases to map the full chain. Your partner tracks which phrases you use.

⚖️Option C

Debate: WiFi vs Ethernet

Your partner argues WiFi is good enough for competitive gaming. Disagree — use cause/effect language to explain exactly why ethernet is better. Then swap sides.

Useful reminders while you speak

  • Start with the symptom ("I'm getting lag / packet loss"), then work backwards to the cause — this mirrors how real troubleshooting works.
  • Use "whenever" for intermittent problems — it shows you've noticed a pattern, not just a one-off event.
  • "That's what's causing it" is your conclusion phrase — don't use it until you've actually identified the problem.
  • Separate cause and effect clearly — if both are blurred together, your explanation will confuse instead of inform.
