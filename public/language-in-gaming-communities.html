Vocabulary
8 words for talking about how communities build their own sense of "us" through language
01
vernacular
NOUN · THE INFORMAL, EVERYDAY LANGUAGE THAT BELONGS TO A PARTICULAR GROUP OR PLACE
often paired with: local vernacular, pick up the vernacular, slip into the vernacular
It's the version of a language that actually gets spoken day to day inside a group — looser, faster, and full of shortcuts that would baffle anyone standing just outside it.
EXAMPLE
"Half the fun of joining a new server is just figuring out the vernacular — what people actually mean when they say 'gg ez' or 'this is rigged.'"
02
shibboleth
NOUN · A WORD OR PHRASE WHOSE PRONUNCIATION OR USE IMMEDIATELY SIGNALS WHETHER SOMEONE BELONGS TO A GROUP
often paired with: act as a shibboleth, a kind of shibboleth, a linguistic shibboleth
It's a small linguistic test that nobody designed on purpose — say the word the "right" way, and people quietly recognize you as one of their own; say it the "wrong" way, and the gap shows immediately.
EXAMPLE
"Saying 'pwned' instead of 'owned' used to be a kind of shibboleth — say it right and people knew you'd been around since the early days."
03
gatekeeping
NOUN · DECIDING, OFTEN UNOFFICIALLY AND UNKINDLY, WHO COUNTS AS A "REAL" MEMBER OF A GROUP
often paired with: pure gatekeeping, accuse someone of gatekeeping, fandom gatekeeping
It's drawing an invisible line around a community and deciding, often loudly, who's allowed to stand inside it — usually based on tests that have very little to do with how much someone actually cares.
EXAMPLE
"There's a fine line between having standards and just gatekeeping — telling someone they're not a 'real' fan because they came in through the movie instead of the books."
04
in-joke
NOUN · A JOKE THAT ONLY MAKES SENSE TO PEOPLE WHO ARE ALREADY PART OF A PARTICULAR GROUP
often paired with: a running in-joke, get the in-joke, an in-joke that's gone too far
It's a joke that does double duty — it's funny, sure, but mostly it works as a quiet little signal that says "you had to be there," which is exactly what makes it feel so warm from the inside and so confusing from the outside.
EXAMPLE
"Half the comments under that clip make no sense unless you already know the in-joke — and honestly, that's kind of the point."
05
code-switch
VERB · TO SHIFT BETWEEN DIFFERENT WAYS OF SPEAKING DEPENDING ON WHO YOU'RE TALKING TO
often paired with: code-switch between, code-switch effortlessly, learn to code-switch
It's the small, often unconscious adjustment people make as they move between different audiences — speaking one way with colleagues, another way with old friends, and a completely different way again the moment they're back in their favorite server.
EXAMPLE
"She code-switches without even noticing it now — technical and precise in the team chat, then completely unhinged the second she's in the meme channel."
06
lexicon
NOUN · THE PARTICULAR STOCK OF WORDS AND EXPRESSIONS THAT BELONG TO A GROUP, FIELD, OR PERSON
often paired with: build a lexicon, enter the lexicon, a shared lexicon
It's the working vocabulary a group quietly assembles over time — partly borrowed, partly invented, and entirely its own — until newcomers realize they're going to need a small glossary just to follow a conversation.
EXAMPLE
"Every guild seems to develop its own little lexicon within a few weeks — words that would mean absolutely nothing to anyone outside it."
07
dog-whistle
NOUN · A PHRASE THAT SOUNDS ORDINARY TO MOST PEOPLE BUT CARRIES A SHARPER, CODED MEANING TO A SPECIFIC AUDIENCE
often paired with: a coded dog-whistle, recognize the dog-whistle, dismiss it as a dog-whistle
It's language built to do two jobs at once — innocent enough to slide past most listeners unnoticed, while landing, fully intended, on the small audience that already knows exactly what's really being said.
EXAMPLE
"What sounded like an innocent joke to most people was actually a dog-whistle — a phrase the in-group recognized instantly, and everyone else just scrolled past."
08
normie
NOUN (INFORMAL, OFTEN DISMISSIVE) · SOMEONE SEEN AS TOO ORDINARY OR MAINSTREAM TO REALLY BELONG TO A NICHE COMMUNITY
often paired with: call someone a normie, normie behavior, "very normie of you"
It's a label a community sometimes reaches for to mark the distance between "us" and "everyone else" — half-joking, occasionally a little sharper than it sounds, and always revealing more about the group doing the labeling than about the person being labeled.
EXAMPLE
"He says it jokingly, but there's always a little edge to it when he calls someone a normie for not knowing the lore."
Language Focus: Modal Verbs of Deduction & Speculation
The quiet art of guessing out loud — how confident you sound depends entirely on which modal you reach for
01
must be
MODAL OF DEDUCTION (PRESENT) · A CONFIDENT CONCLUSION BASED ON THE EVIDENCE IN FRONT OF YOU
often paired with: must be joking, must be a smurf, that must be exhausting
It's the modal you reach for when the evidence basically leaves you no other option — not a wild guess, but the conclusion that simply makes the most sense of everything you're seeing.
EXAMPLE
"They must be a smurf — nobody plays like that in their first week."
02
can't have been
MODAL OF DEDUCTION (PAST, NEGATIVE) · CONFIDENTLY RULING SOMETHING OUT ABOUT THE PAST
often paired with: can't have been easy, can't have been their first time, can't have been a coincidence
It's the mirror image of "must have been" — the moment you look back at something and decide, with real confidence, that it simply could not have happened the way it first appeared to.
EXAMPLE
"That can't have been their first time in this raid; they knew exactly where to stand."
03
might just be
MODAL OF SPECULATION · A SOFT, LOW-COMMITMENT GUESS THAT LEAVES PLENTY OF ROOM TO BE WRONG
often paired with: might just be tired, might just be nerves, might just be a fluke
It's the gentlest way to float a possibility — not a claim, barely even a guess, just a small "here's one thing it could be" offered without much weight behind it at all.
EXAMPLE
"She might just be having an off night — I wouldn't read too much into one bad game."
04
may have
MODAL OF SPECULATION (PAST) · A FAIR, EVEN-HANDED GUESS ABOUT WHAT POSSIBLY HAPPENED
often paired with: may have missed it, may have muted the chat, may have misread the situation
It's the modal of giving someone the benefit of the doubt — naming a possible explanation for what they did, without ever pretending you actually know what really went on in their head.
EXAMPLE
"He may have muted the chat the second it started getting toxic, to be fair to him."
05
should have been
MODAL OF EXPECTATION (UNMET) · POINTING OUT THE GAP BETWEEN WHAT WAS EXPECTED AND WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENED
often paired with: should have been easy, should have been over by now, should have been more careful
It's the phrase that quietly carries a little disappointment inside it — naming the outcome that, by any reasonable measure, really ought to have happened, and didn't.
EXAMPLE
"Honestly, this should have been an easy win for a team that's been practicing for months."
06
would have seen
MODAL OF HYPOTHETICAL DEDUCTION (PAST) · WHAT WOULD CLEARLY HAVE BEEN OBVIOUS TO SOMEONE IN THE RIGHT POSITION
often paired with: would have seen it coming, would have known better, would have noticed
It's the modal for pointing backward at a missed signal — laying out, with a little hindsight, exactly what someone in the right position would surely have picked up on, if only they'd been paying attention.
EXAMPLE
"Anyone who'd actually read the patch notes would have seen that nerf coming a mile away."
07
can't help but
EXPRESSION OF INVOLUNTARY REACTION · SOMETHING YOU DO ALMOST AUTOMATICALLY, WHETHER YOU MEAN TO OR NOT
often paired with: can't help but laugh, can't help but wonder, can't help but notice
It's the phrase for the reaction that just slips out — the laugh, the eye-roll, the little gasp — happening on its own, faster than you could ever decide whether to hold it back.
EXAMPLE
"I can't help but laugh every time someone tries that strategy — it never, ever works."
08
seem to
VERB OF IMPRESSION · DESCRIBING HOW SOMETHING APPEARS, WHILE QUIETLY LEAVING ROOM FOR DOUBT
often paired with: seem to know, seem to enjoy, seem to think
It's a softening verb — a way of describing your impression of something while gently signaling that it's only an impression, and that you're leaving the door open in case the full picture turns out to be different.
EXAMPLE
"They seem to know everyone in the server already, which is a little intimidating for a newcomer."
In Use
Watch the same ideas about belonging and language shift register — from a sociolinguistics lecture, to a community-manager panel, to a server chat at 1am
Lecture / SociolinguisticsOn the Construction of In-Group Identity

"Online gaming communities offer a remarkably compact case study in how groups construct linguistic identity. Within weeks, a new server develops its own vernacular — abbreviations, in-jokes, half-borrowed slang — and that vernacular quickly hardens into something closer to a shibboleth: a small, almost invisible test that separates those who belong from those who merely visit. What looks, from the outside, like nonsense or noise is, on closer inspection, an entire social architecture, built and maintained largely through word choice alone."

Notice the careful, observational distance — "offers a case study," "on closer inspection." The lecture register names the mechanism without ever stepping inside it; the speaker studies belonging from a comfortable arm's length.
Community Manager PanelPanel — "How to Read a Server's Health"

"Honestly, you can tell how healthy a community is just by listening to how people talk to newcomers. In the good servers, someone will gently translate the lexicon for you — 'oh, when they say that, they just mean...' — and you feel welcomed in. In the rough ones, it's pure gatekeeping: somebody must be testing you, asking questions they already know the answer to, just waiting for you to slip up so they can dismiss you as a normie. Same words, completely different intentions underneath them."

A confident, conversational register — "honestly," "you can tell" — that mixes a real observation with a small live deduction ("somebody must be testing you") right in the middle of explaining the pattern. The speaker performs the very habit of reading a room that they're describing.
Server ChatThread — "two months in and still lost"

"ok genuine question — how long was everyone else lurking before they actually felt like they could post in this server without getting roasted?? i've been here like two months and i STILL can't tell which jokes are in-jokes and which ones are just. things people say. someone explain the whole 'goblin mode' thing to me i feel like i missed some kind of orientation lol"

Compare this to the lecture above — the exact same experience (decoding a group's vernacular from the outside) but lived from the inside, in real time, with no analytical distance at all. Capitals, run-on questions, and "lol" do the emotional work that "on closer inspection" did up there.
Register & Stance
How speculation changes shape across contexts — from the careful hedge of a lecture to the blunt certainty of a chat message at midnight
ContextTypical StanceExample
Sociolinguistics lecture
Formal, analytical, hedged		 Highly formal "This pattern would, in most cases, be best understood as a marker of group identity rather than mere shorthand."
Community guidelines post
Careful, official, even-handed		 Highly formal "Such language may be perceived as exclusionary, even when no harm is intended by the person using it."
Community manager panel
Conversational, confident, a little blunt		 Semi-formal "Honestly, you can usually tell within five minutes whether a server's gatekeeping or just having fun."
In-server voice chat
Informal, quick, often joking		 Semi-formal "Wait, they must be new — nobody who's been here a week says 'good game' unironically."
Comments / forum thread
Informal, blunt, fast and certain		 Informal / emotional "ok that HAS to be a bot, there's no way a real person types that fast"
✅The same guess can be dressed in very different clothes depending on where it's made — "this would, in most cases, suggest..." in a lecture and "ok that HAS to be a bot" in a comment thread are doing the exact same job of speculating, just at opposite ends of the confidence dial.
💡Watch how formal registers tend to soften their guesses — "may be perceived as," "would, in most cases" — while informal ones often do the opposite, reaching for "must," "has to be," and "no way" to make a guess sound like a verdict.
⚠️"Confident-sounding" and "correct" aren't the same thing. A chat message that says someone "must be smurfing" carries exactly the same evidential weight as a lecture that hedges with "this would suggest" — the certainty is just dialed up for effect.
❌Don't let a blunt modal fool you into thinking the speaker actually knows more than they do. "Has to be," "no way," and "100% must be" often just mean "I'm fairly sure" said loudly — translating them down a register can be a useful gut-check.

Two Months In: What Nobody Tells You About Joining a New Server
A short forum post — fill in the blanks with words from the bank above, then check your answers

When I first joined, I genuinely thought I was bad at the game. Turns out I was actually just bad at the click to fill — half the callouts in voice chat made no sense to me, and everyone else seemed to understand them instantly, like they'd all gone to the same school I'd somehow missed.

The first week felt like a wall of pure click to fill. Someone would ask me a question I clearly didn't know the answer to, then sigh like I'd failed some test I never knew I was taking. It took me a while to realize that wasn't actually about me at all — it was just how that particular person treated anyone new.

What helped more than anything was finding one person willing to quietly walk me through the click to fill — what "ratio'd" meant, why everyone kept typing "F," that sort of thing. Within a week I went from confused outsider to laughing at an click to fill for the first time, and it honestly felt like a small personal victory.

I've also noticed I click to fill without even trying now — I talk one way in this server and a completely different way with my actual friends, and it took me a minute to even notice I was doing it. There's apparently one phrase that works as a kind of click to fill here too — say it the way the regulars do, and people relax around you almost instantly.

The one thing I'd warn newcomers about: every so often someone drops what sounds like an offhand joke that's actually a click to fill aimed at a much smaller, much less friendly audience — and most people in the server won't even clock it. And yes, I did get called a click to fill exactly once, on day three, and I'm choosing to wear it as a badge of honor.

🗣 Further Discussion

1
Every community seems to develop its own shibboleths — small words or habits that quietly sort "us" from "them." Can you think of one from a group you belong to, and how someone new would ever learn it?
2
Is gatekeeping ever actually useful — a way of protecting something a community has built — or is it always, in the end, just exclusion wearing a more respectable name?
3
Notice how often people reach for "must be" instead of "might be" when they're talking about strangers online. What does that small choice of modal verb reveal about how much benefit of the doubt we extend to people we've never met?
4
Code-switching between a server's vernacular and "normal" speech happens almost automatically for a lot of people. Have you ever caught yourself doing it — and did it feel natural, or oddly like translating?
5
"Normie" can be affectionate, dismissive, or somewhere in between depending on tone and context. How much do you think a single label like that can shape whether someone feels welcome in a space — or quietly decides to leave it?
